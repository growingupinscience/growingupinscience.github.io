### Official Story
Shane received his Bachelors of Science (Hons) and of Biomedical Science from the University of Melbourne, majoring in Neuroscience and Anatomy & Cell Biology. He completed his PhD in Pharmacology in the lab of Norman Saunders and Katarzyna Dziegielewska, studying the development of the blood-CSF barrier to see if they might be leveraged to deliver drugs to the brain. For his postdoc, Shane moved to Stanford University to work with Ben Barres, where he shifted his focus to astrocytes. He discovered signals from microglia that can induce a neurotoxic form of reactive astrocyte that contributes to neuron death in multiple neurodegenerative diseases – resulting in a paper cited over 8,000 times (!). In 2018, he started his own lab at NYU Grossman School of Medicine, where he is now an Associate Professor in the Institute for Translational Neuroscience with joint appointments in the Departments of Neuroscience and Ophthalmology, and co‑Director of the Parekh Center for Interdisciplinary Neurology. His research uses rodent and human iPSC-based models to define reactive astrocyte substates and uncover how they influence neuronal survival and circuit function, with the goal of shifting astrocyte responses from harmful to protective in disorders of the aging and injured brain. Shane’s work has been recognized with awards including the Glenn Foundation Award for Aging, the Inge Grundke‑Iqbal Award from the Alzheimer’s Association, the David Hague Early Career Investigator of the Year Award from Alzheimer’s Research UK, and the Pershing Square MIND Prize. He is also an academic founder of the biotech startup AstronauTx Ltd. and co‑Editor in Chief of the journal GLIA.

### Unofficial Story
Shane grew up in Manjimup, a small farming town in southwest Australia, four hours from the nearest city. Because his high school offered no advanced science or math classes on site, he had to take them remotely, which meant spending much of his school day studying alone in a windowless room with classmates more than a dozen hours’ drive away. After grade 10 he rejoined regular classes, and devoted at least as much time to trying to keep up with state‑level field hockey as to studying (it was his sister that ended up an Olympian, but there was fierce competition!).
He briefly pursued the idea of medicine before realizing he would have a horrible bedside manner, and left his home town for what was meant to be a two‑week holiday in Melbourne, but he stayed for a decade. First he juggled full‑time work in a 1,000‑seat, 24‑hour pizza restaurant while pursuing an undergraduate science degree in math, physics, and chemistry, until he was mugged late one night on his way home, diagnosed with a ruptured aneurysm, and had emergency brain surgery. Seeing his own brain convinced him that neuroscience was more interesting than physics, but concentrating was harder than before, so he dropped out of university and opened a restaurant. But he returned to science 18 months later to enroll in a neuroscience program while still working ~40 hours a week – often studying in the kitchen, and cooking when the chef didn’t show up. When the restaurant finally closed, he pivoted to waiting tables at a wedding venue and ended up launching his own wedding‑planning business that at its peak ran five weddings per weekend while he finished his degree.
In 2005, hoping to do a PhD in pharmacology, Shane went door‑to‑door asking faculty to take him on and was repeatedly told he was “not smart enough for a PhD” (not helped by having failed genetics three times during his post‑surgery fog). A husband‑and‑wife team, Norman Saunders and Katarzyna Dziegielewska, eventually took a chance on him, giving him a scientific home and an extraordinary network. In their lab he shared an office with Geoffrey Burnstock (each summer), and was sent to San Antonio (for work on primates  and opossums) and Albany (to study stem cell and molecular biology with Sally Temple) in the cooler months. At a Cold Spring Harbor meeting he happened to sit next to a slightly odd but compelling scientist from Stanford, Ben Barres. They fell into “scientific love”; only later, after visiting Ben’s lab on a stopover flight home, did Shane realize he’d been on a job interview.
A year later, in 2012, after Ben’s 45‑person lab had space, Shane arrived at Stanford with two suitcases and just enough cash for a week in a hotel. The lab felt like a scientific utopia: lots of scientific freedom and resources, and a constant flow of brilliant, generous people. Early in his postdoc, just as his project was taking off, Shane was hit by a car while cycling, shattering knees, pelvis, elbows, ribs, eye socket, and his left wrist. Multiple surgeries, a two‑year court case, and years of physical therapy followed. Unable to move his fingers for over a year, he used the enforced slowdown to write grants and build a small team. Some very productive years followed.
Then in 2016 Ben was diagnosed with pancreatic cancer. As Shane’s project was relatively mature, he often stepped in as Ben’s proxy to speak at major meetings – on one memorable occasion at a fundraising dinner at the Hollywood Hilton his lecture was opened by a performance by KISS. His main paper still wasn’t published when he went on the job market early, relying on Ben’s network rather than preprints to open doors. Several offers later, he chose NYU Grossman School of Medicine. He moved to New York in early 2018, only 2 months prior, Ben had passed away.  Shane started his lab, helped found a London‑based biotech company and several foundations, and continues to surround himself with trainees he’s confident will surpass him as quickly as possible – which, for him, is the entire point.
